A1 1. Articles — le, la, l', les · un, une, des · du, de la

Every French noun has a gender (masculine or feminine) and a number (singular or plural). The article in front of the noun changes to match both. There are three families of articles: definite, indefinite, and partitive.

1.1 Definite articles — "the"

FormUsed forExamples
lemasculine singular, consonant startle livre, le garçon, le chat
lafeminine singular, consonant startla table, la fille, la maison
l'singular before vowel or mute h (m. or f.)l'ami, l'amie, l'eau, l'hôtel
lesplural (any gender)les livres, les tables, les amis

Le chat dort sur la table.The cat is sleeping on the table.

L'enfant joue avec les amis de sa sœur.The child is playing with his/her sister's friends.

Use the definite article in three situations:

  1. A specific, known noun: le livre que tu m'as donné — the book you gave me.
  2. A generality (English drops the article here): J'aime le café. — I like coffee.
  3. Countries, languages, dates, days (when habitual): la France, le français, le lundi (on Mondays).

1.2 Indefinite articles — "a / an / some"

FormUsed forExamples
unmasculine singularun homme, un problème, un livre
unefeminine singularune femme, une idée, une voiture
desplural (any gender)des hommes, des idées, des livres

J'ai un chien et une chatte.I have a dog and a (female) cat.

Il y a des enfants dans le jardin.There are (some) children in the garden.

1.3 Partitive articles — "some / any" for uncountable things

French uses a special article when you mean an unspecified quantity of something — typically food, drink, or abstract qualities. English usually omits this ("I drink coffee") but French requires it ("I drink some coffee").

FormUsed forExamples
dumasculine singulardu pain, du vin, du courage
de lafeminine singularde la salade, de la patience
de l'before vowel or mute hde l'eau, de l'huile
desplural (countable)des pâtes, des frites

Je voudrais du pain et de la confiture.I'd like (some) bread and (some) jam.

Tu bois de l'eau ?Do you drink water?

1.4 The big rule: negation turns everything into de / d'

After a negation (ne … pas), all indefinite and partitive articles become de (or d' before a vowel). Definite articles do not change.

AffirmativeNegative
J'ai un stylo.Je n'ai pas de stylo.
Il mange de la viande.Il ne mange pas de viande.
Nous avons des enfants.Nous n'avons pas d'enfants.
J'aime le café.Je n'aime pas le café. (no change!)
Exception: after être, partitives/indefinites keep their normal form even in negation. Ce n'est pas un problème. — It's not a problem.

1.5 Contractions: à and de + definite article

When the prepositions à (to/at) or de (of/from) meet le or les, they fuse:

Preposition+ le+ la+ l'+ les
àauà laà l'aux
dedude lade l'des

Je vais au cinéma.I'm going to the cinema. (à + le → au)

Elle parle aux enfants.She's talking to the children. (à + les → aux)

C'est la voiture du voisin.It's the neighbour's car. (de + le → du)

A1 2. Subject pronouns

Every conjugated French verb needs a subject. Subject pronouns are the "doer" of the action.

Who it refers toPronounNotes

you (one person, informal)tufriends, family, children, animals, peers
he / a masculine thingilany masculine noun or person
she / a feminine thingelleany feminine noun or person

we (the group I belong to)nousformal or written; spoken French prefers on

2.1 The chameleon pronoun on

On always takes a 3rd-person singular verb (on est, on a, on parle) but has three meanings depending on context:

  1. "We" in everyday speech — much more common than nous: On y va ? — Shall we go?
  2. "One / people in general": En France, on mange à 20 heures. — In France, people eat at 8 pm.
  3. "They (unknown)": On a sonné à la porte. — Someone rang the bell.

2.2 tu vs vous — when to use which

Use tuUse vous
friends, family, kids, animalsstrangers, older people, professionals
colleagues your age (in many workplaces)customers, doctors, teachers (in formal settings)
online forums, social mediabusiness correspondence, polite requests
When unsure, start with vous. The other person may invite you to tu with the phrase On peut se tutoyer ? — Can we use "tu"?

2.3 il/elle for things, not just people

French has no gender-neutral "it". Every noun is masculine or feminine, so the pronoun is il or elle based on the noun's gender.

Où est la voiture ? — Elle est dans le garage.Where is the car? — It's in the garage. (la voiture is feminine)

Tu vois le livre ? — Oui, il est sur la table.Do you see the book? — Yes, it's on the table.

A1 3. The verb être (to be)

The most important verb in French. Used for identity, profession, location, and state. Also serves as an auxiliary for some past-tense constructions (chapter 21).

3.1 Conjugation in the present tense

PersonFormSounds likeExample
jesuis/sɥi/Je suis étudiant.
tues/ɛ/Tu es français.
il / elle / onest/ɛ/Elle est médecin.
noussommes/sɔm/Nous sommes à Paris.
vousêtes/ɛt/Vous êtes prêt ?
ils / ellessont/sɔ̃/Ils sont fatigués.
tu es and il est sound identical (both /ɛ/). Only the pronoun distinguishes them in speech.

3.2 Five core uses

UseExampleTranslation
Identity / nameJe suis Marie.I am Marie.
Profession (no article!)Il est professeur.He is a teacher.
Nationality (no article!)Elle est belge.She is Belgian.
LocationNous sommes à la maison.We are at home.
State / feelingJe suis fatigué(e).I am tired.
For professions and nationalities, French drops the article after être. Je suis médecin. — I am a doctor. — no un. But with c'est you do need the article: C'est un médecin.

3.3 With adjectives — agreement is mandatory

Adjectives following être must match the subject's gender and number:

SubjectForm
Il (m.sg)Il est grand.
Elle (f.sg)Elle est grande.
Ils (m.pl / mixed)Ils sont grands.
Elles (f.pl)Elles sont grandes.

A1 4. The verb avoir (to have)

The second pillar of French. Used for possession, age, and several physical/emotional states where English uses "to be". Also the most common auxiliary in past-tense constructions.

4.1 Conjugation in the present tense

PersonFormSounds likeExample
jeai (j'ai)/e/J'ai un frère.
tuas/a/Tu as raison.
il / elle / ona/a/Elle a vingt ans.
nousavons/a.vɔ̃/Nous avons faim.
vousavez/a.ve/Vous avez l'heure ?
ils / ellesont/ɔ̃/Ils ont deux chats.
Don't confuse ont (they have, /ɔ̃/) with sont (they are, /sɔ̃/) — single consonant difference, both nasal.

4.2 The "avoir" expressions — where English uses "to be"

French treats these states as things you have, not things you are:

FrenchLiteralActual meaning
J'ai 25 ans.I have 25 years.I am 25 years old.
J'ai faim.I have hunger.I am hungry.
J'ai soif.I have thirst.I am thirsty.
J'ai chaud.I have hot.I am hot.
J'ai froid.I have cold.I am cold.
J'ai peur.I have fear.I am afraid.
J'ai sommeil.I have sleep.I am sleepy.
J'ai raison / tort.I have right / wrong.I am right / wrong.
J'ai besoin de …I have need of …I need …
J'ai envie de …I have desire of …I feel like …
J'ai mal à la tête.I have hurt to the head.I have a headache.
When you want to say "I'm hungry", your instinct as an English speaker is to reach for être. Resist. J'ai faim, never je suis faim.

4.3 il y a — "there is / there are"

This invariable expression uses avoir in the 3rd singular. It does not change in the plural.

Il y a un chat dans le jardin.There is a cat in the garden.

Il y a trois enfants ici.There are three children here.

Il n'y a pas de problème.There is no problem.

A1 5. Regular -er verbs · présent

Roughly 80% of all French verbs end in -er and follow the same predictable pattern. Master this one rule and you can conjugate thousands of verbs.

5.1 The pattern

Take the infinitive (e.g. parler — to speak), drop the -er to get the stem (parl-), then add these endings:

PersonEndingparler (to speak)aimer (to love)habiter (to live)
je-eje parlej'aimej'habite
tu-estu parlestu aimestu habites
il / elle / on-eil parleelle aimeon habite
nous-onsnous parlonsnous aimonsnous habitons
vous-ezvous parlezvous aimezvous habitez
ils / elles-entils parlentelles aimentils habitent
The endings -e, -es, -e, -ent are all silent. Je parle, tu parles, il parle, ils parlent all sound the same — only the subject pronoun tells them apart.

5.2 Spelling variants — small but important

Verbs ending in -ger (manger, voyager, nager)

Insert an extra e before -ons to keep the soft /ʒ/ sound:

manger → nous mangeonswe eat
voyager → nous voyageonswe travel

Verbs ending in -cer (commencer, lancer)

The c becomes ç before -ons:

commencer → nous commençonswe begin
lancer → nous lançonswe throw

Verbs ending in -yer (payer, envoyer, essayer)

The y becomes i in the four "shoe" forms (je/tu/il/ils):

payer → je paie, tu paies, il paie, ils paient
payer → nous payons, vous payez

Verbs with stem e + consonant + -er (acheter, lever, mener)

Add a grave accent (è) on the stem in the shoe forms:

acheter → j'achète, tu achètes, il achète, ils achètent
acheter → nous achetons, vous achetez
lever → je lève, nous levons

Verbs ending in -eler / -eter (appeler, jeter)

Most double the consonant in the shoe forms:

appeler → j'appelle, tu appelles, il appelle, ils appellent
appeler → nous appelons, vous appelez
jeter → je jette, nous jetons

5.3 The one truly irregular -er verb: aller (to go)

It looks -er but it's not regular — chapter 24 covers it in full. Quick form:

je vais · tu vas · il va · nous allons · vous allez · ils vont

A1 6. Negation — ne … pas

French negation is a two-word sandwich around the verb. The English "I don't speak French" is in French "I not speak not French".

6.1 The basic rule

AffirmativeNegative
Je parle français.Je ne parle pas français.
Il vient ce soir.Il ne vient pas ce soir.
Nous travaillons le dimanche.Nous ne travaillons pas le dimanche.

6.2 ne → n' before a vowel or mute h

J'aime ça.Je n'aime pas ça.
Il habite ici.Il n'habite pas ici.

6.3 Negation drops the indefinite/partitive article

After a negative verb, un, une, des, du, de la, de l' all become de (or d' before a vowel). Reviewed already in §1.4 — it's worth seeing twice:

AffirmativeNegative
J'ai un chien.Je n'ai pas de chien.
Il mange du pain.Il ne mange pas de pain.
Elle a des amis.Elle n'a pas d'amis.

6.4 Other negative pairs (preview of A2)

NegationMeaningExample
ne … jamaisneverJe ne mange jamais de viande. — I never eat meat.
ne … riennothingIl ne fait rien. — He's doing nothing.
ne … personnenobodyJe ne vois personne. — I don't see anyone.
ne … plusno longer / not anymoreElle n'habite plus ici. — She doesn't live here anymore.
ne … pas encorenot yetJe n'ai pas encore mangé. — I haven't eaten yet.
ne … queonlyJe n'ai qu'un euro. — I only have one euro.
In everyday spoken French, the ne is often dropped: Je sais pas. C'est pas vrai. Don't do this in writing or formal speech — only listen for it. In written French the ne is mandatory.

A1 7. Forming questions

French has three ways to turn a statement into a question. They mean the same thing but differ in register (how formal).

7.1 The three registers

MethodRegisterExample
1. Intonation (rising tone)casual / spokenTu viens ?
2. Est-ce que + statementstandard / neutralEst-ce que tu viens ?
3. Verb-subject inversionformal / writtenViens-tu ?
As a learner, default to est-ce que. It works in every situation, doesn't require inversion gymnastics, and is universally understood. Reserve inversion for written French and very polite speech.

7.2 est-ce que — the safe choice

Drop "est-ce que" at the front of any statement and you have a yes/no question. It elides to est-ce qu' before a vowel.

Est-ce que tu parles français ?Do you speak French?

Est-ce qu'il vient ce soir ?Is he coming tonight?

Est-ce que vous avez l'heure ?Do you have the time?

7.3 Inversion — formal style

Flip the subject and verb, joined with a hyphen:

StatementInversion
Tu parles français.Parles-tu français ?
Vous avez faim.Avez-vous faim ?
Il est médecin.Est-il médecin ?
When the verb ends in a vowel and the subject is il/elle/on, insert -t- for euphony: Parle-t-il français ? · A-t-elle vingt ans ? · Va-t-on partir ?

7.4 Inversion with a noun subject

When the subject is a noun (not a pronoun), keep the noun first and add an inverted pronoun:

Marie parle-t-elle espagnol ?Does Marie speak Spanish?

Vos amis habitent-ils à Lyon ?Do your friends live in Lyon?

7.5 Question + word order with question words

All three methods extend to wh-questions (see chapter 8). With est-ce que the question word goes first:

Où est-ce que tu habites ?Where do you live? (standard)

Où habites-tu ?Where do you live? (formal, with inversion)

Tu habites où ?Where do you live? (casual, question word at the end)

8.2 qu'est-ce que vs qui est-ce qui

For "what" or "who" as subject vs object, French distinguishes carefully:

RolePerson (who)Thing (what)
SubjectQui est-ce qui … ? · Qui … ?Qu'est-ce qui … ?

8.3 Question word + preposition

Where English ends with a stranded preposition ("Who are you talking to?"), French puts the preposition first:

À qui parles-tu ?Who are you talking to?

De quoi parlez-vous ?What are you talking about?

Avec qui sors-tu ce soir ?Who are you going out with tonight?

A1 9. Noun gender and plurals

11.2 The English-speaker trap: son / sa / ses

In English you say "his book" or "her book" depending on the owner's gender. In French you say son livre or sa voiture depending on the noun's gender — son/sa/ses means both "his" and "her".

A1 12. Demonstrative adjectives — ce, cet, cette, ces

French has one demonstrative adjective covering both "this" and "that" — the same word, four forms by gender + number + vowel-start.

FormUsed forExample
cemasc-sg + consonantce livre, ce garçon, ce chat
cetmasc-sg + vowel or mute hcet ami, cet homme, cet été
cettefem-sg (always)cette femme, cette idée, cette école
cesplural (any gender)ces livres, ces idées, ces amis
cet and cette sound identical (/sɛt/). The difference is purely visual — cet for masculine, cette for feminine.

12.1 "This" vs "that" — add -ci or -là

If you need to distinguish "this one here" from "that one there", attach -ci (closer) or -là (farther) to the noun with a hyphen:

Je préfère ce livre-ci, pas ce livre-là.I prefer this book, not that book.

Cette robe-ci est plus jolie que cette robe-là.This dress is prettier than that dress.

In daily speech, French speakers rarely bother with -ci/-là unless explicitly contrasting — bare ce/cet/cette/ces covers both "this" and "that".

12.2 The pronoun ça / cela / ceci

To say "this" or "that" as a pronoun (without a noun), use ça (everyday) or cela (formal). Ceci exists but is rare.

Qu'est-ce que c'est ? — C'est ça.What is that? — That's it.

Ça ne marche pas.That doesn't work.

Je préfère cela.I prefer that. (formal)

These short impersonal expressions get used dozens of times a day. The trick is knowing which one to pick — they're not interchangeable.

Existential. Invariable in the present (it does not change for plural).

Il y a un chat dans le jardin.There is a cat in the garden.

Used to identify or describe with an article + noun, or with a name, pronoun, or general adjective.

Je leur ai parlé.I spoke to them. (no agreement — parlé stays unchanged)

26.5 Watch out: à + thing → use y, not COI

The COI pronouns (lui, leur) replace only à + person. For à + thing, use y (next chapter):

Je réponds à Marie. → Je lui réponds.(à Marie = person → lui)

Je réponds à la question. → J'y réponds.(à la question = thing → y)

26.6 Exception: certain verbs of thinking about a person keep à

For penser à, faire attention à, s'intéresser à, songer à with a person, French uses the stressed pronouns (à moi, à toi, à lui, à elle, à eux, à elles), not lui/leur:

Je pense à Marie. → Je pense à elle.I'm thinking about her. (not lui)

36.7 Uvular R — the most distinctive sound

The French r is made at the back of the throat (uvular), not on the tongue tip. Approximate: a soft gargle. Practice with words like Paris, rue, frère, rouge. English speakers often substitute their tongue-tip R — comprehensible but obviously foreign.

36.8 The H — always silent

French H is never pronounced. It comes in two flavors:

  • Mute H (h muet): acts like a vowel — triggers elision and liaison: l'homme, les‿hommes.
  • Aspirated H (h aspiré): historically borrowed words. Blocks elision and liaison: le héros (not l'héros), les / héros (no liaison).

Common aspirated-H words: le héros, le hibou, le haricot, la honte, la hauteur, le hasard, le hockey, dehors. Dictionaries mark them with a † or apostrophe.

36.9 Stress — barely any

French is largely stress-free. Each syllable gets roughly equal weight, with a slight emphasis on the last syllable of a word or phrase. Avoid English stress-timed rhythm: "PA-ris" (English) becomes "pa-RIS" (French).
